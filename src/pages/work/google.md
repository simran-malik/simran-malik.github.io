## How I Worked

* **Owning the product.** For nearly all my projects, I took on the Product Manager role, gathering requirements and worked across Business, UX, Tax, Legal and third-party vendors to define what we built.
* **Leading engineers.** I scoped projects, ran design reviews and reviewed code for two engineers on my team.
* **Shipping carefully.** I rolled features out gradually, watched A/B metrics, and rolled back when the numbers didn’t hold up. On systems handling millions of daily orders, being able to undo a change mattered as much as shipping it.
* **Debugging the hard way.** This was before AI coding agents, so I traced bugs by hand through unfamiliar codebases and hunted down edge cases myself. That built the instinct I still rely on for figuring out where a system will break.

## How I Grew

* **Redesigning is harder than building from scratch.** You inherit undocumented edge cases and millions of users who can’t afford a regression.
* **Get decision-makers in one room.** Aligning stakeholders one by one is slow and drifts. Putting everyone together to settle a decision is usually faster and holds better.
* **Under a hard deadline, use existing expertise.** On a high-priority project, it’s better to rearrange the priorities of the person who already knows the technology than to give the work to someone who has to learn it first. Growth opportunities belong on projects that can absorb the ramp-up time.
* **Whiteboard.** For early design discussions, a whiteboard gets people agreeing much faster than a long document. The doc is for recording what was decided.
* **Good engineering doesn’t guarantee business results.** Not every project I shipped hit its targets, even when the engineering was solid. I learned to check the business assumptions early, so problems show up before launch rather than after.
